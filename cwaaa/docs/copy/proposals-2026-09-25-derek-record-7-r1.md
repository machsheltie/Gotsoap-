**The engine.** Derek’s premise is that *a date accepted is proof*. The sniff test runs on the
clothes, never the body; Axe is baseline and Febreze is the elevated measure; women who leave are
unrelated exceptions; and the booth he stands in four nights a week is a room where nobody talks to
him, which he reads as a room where nobody complains. The record is meticulous up to the Office
fragment, and then it stops.

## 5 · Testimony

> “I check my clothes before I put them on. If they smell fine, they’re fine.
>
> If I’m going out, I use Axe. If it’s a date, I do a little more. That’s what the Febreze is for.
>
> I don’t use deodorant. Never have. It has never come up.
>
> I’m still getting dates. Nobody seems to include that part. If this was the problem they say it is,
> women wouldn’t keep agreeing to go out with me.
>
> Some of them don’t work out. That happens to everybody. One had a family emergency. One wasn’t
> feeling well and left before dinner. I don’t know what either of those has to do with how often I
> shower.
>
> I’m in that booth four nights a week with people standing right in front of me, and not one person
> has ever said a word to me about it.
>
> I know what clean clothes smell like. I check.”

The testimony opens and closes on the same claim. The sniff test is a closed proof system: it can
only return the answer it started with, and every disconfirming event gets filed as unrelated.

`PARTICIPANT PRESENT: YES` is the record’s afterimage, and the reason to recommend it. CWAAA has just
spent four rows establishing that it could not reach him: number blocked, letter unclaimed, no further
contact available. The Office’s document says he was there. Nothing accounts for the difference, and
nothing should. `NO` makes the Office merely unsuccessful; a bar makes it coy. `YES` says nothing at
all and means everything.

sequence, no letter suffix. Later than Billy Bob’s, which the dates support.
